Visible terrain forms are uneven in quality. Several freestanding rock towers have convincing irregular silhouettes, particularly behind the fork and finish. However, the large salt-run and launch mesas have broad sloping skirts, almost straight vertical folds, sharp triangular upper facets, and horizontally repeated surface marks. Those forms read differently from the target's stacked shelves, undercuts, fractured slabs, and talus. More fine surface texture alone would not correct the mismatch.

The detail hierarchy currently jumps from very large mountains or tanks to tiny surface marks, bolts, poles, and occasional isolated rocks. The missing middle includes broken cliff shelves, buttresses, rockfall fans, shoulder berms, clusters of equipment, tank service assemblies, pipes changing direction around supports, and large worn panels. Small details therefore do not accumulate into a convincing finished place. The target uses large shapes to establish the scene, medium forms to explain its construction, and small marks to finish those forms.

The depth gap is particularly obvious across the launch sequence. Approach and crest show a broad empty ridge with two boulders and route poles against a distant factory. The descent finally reveals a leftward roadway, but the land between the road and the horizon still feels vacant. There is some distance color separation; the stronger missing ingredient is a chain of overlapping terrain forms at different distances. The target's valley has several readable height levels and landforms connecting the near drop to the industrial destination.

The fork ambiguity is visible in all three supplied angles. The elevated left deck and lower left-turning road are present, but the deck begins as a thin slab with several edge segments occupying the junction. At entry, the vehicle overlaps the deck edge in the image. After entry, a deck curb runs diagonally beneath the central craft while the right engine remains visually over the lower roadway. This is not a claim of collision or geometry failure; it is evidence that the legal lanes and the intended point of commitment are visually unclear. The reference presents a massive central rock divider and two clearly separated trajectories long before commitment.

The craft's paint wear and small hardware are visible, so it is beyond a plain untextured blockout. However, each engine remains a largely uninterrupted tapered shell with a bright flat cyan end ring. There is little visible layered turbine depth, asymmetric service hardware, thick connected hoses, or a clear hierarchy of armor, frame, and exposed machinery. The central pilot is not legible at this camera. In `garage.png`, the typography, spacing, and color system are coherent, but the hero craft occupies too little of its large inspection area to sell its finish or make the pilot readable. The garage interface cannot compensate for the race-camera art gap. `live-drive.png` repeats the sparse terrain and vehicle observations; its speed readout does not demonstrate speed sensation.

The five highest-impact visual changes are:

1. **Replace the smooth folded mesa forms with visibly layered terrain.** Build broad shelves, undercut bands, broken vertical faces, shoulder talus, and several differently scaled outcrops into the salt-run and launch views. Preserve the useful irregular spire silhouettes, but stop applying the same fine striation as the main explanation of every rock surface. The large landforms must read correctly before extra texture is added.
2. **Recompose the fork and launch around visible route continuity.** Give the fork a substantial central divider, a clear upper-route entrance, and separate continuous shoulders for the lower lane. Show the upper route continuing beyond its crest. At launch, expose enough of the descent or landing corridor from the approach to make the intended trajectory legible, then extend that route visually into the valley.
3. **Finish the racer and make the pilot unmistakable at the actual race camera.** Use visibly layered engine rims and internal machinery, distinct structural and armor surfaces, stronger worn panel edges, substantial cables, and a readable seated pilot silhouette with head, shoulders, and controls. Adjust camera/vehicle framing so the finished vehicle anchors the lower frame without concealing the route. A different vehicle design is acceptable; the target's degree of finish and pilot readability are the requirement.
4. **Fill the missing medium scale with clustered, site-specific forms.** At grid, add canopy masses, service-bay interiors, stacked machinery, gantry fittings, and people-sized objects. In foundry, give tanks functional bases, valves, braced pipe connections, service platforms, and distinct clusters instead of evenly spaced plain cylinders. Along natural routes, compose shelves, fallen slabs, eroded gullies, and debris banks rather than more isolated copies of the same boulder.
5. **Stage foreground, middle distance, and horizon as one composition.** Introduce stronger near shoulder framing and road wear that follows the route, use intermediate terrain to overlap and lead toward landmarks, and reduce far-field contrast/detail selectively. Give each location a deliberate light-and-shadow focal path. The sunset palette is already serviceable; the next gain should come from form, value structure, and depth rather than a stronger global color wash.
